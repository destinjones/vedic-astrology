How it works

Every chart on this site comes from the same pipeline, run in your browser, in this order. Nothing is looked up from a table of pre-made charts and nothing is sent to a server. If you want to redo any step yourself, the check page prints each of these numbers for your own chart.

The receipts

A real ephemeris

Positions come from the Swiss Ephemeris, the same library behind Astro.com, Jagannatha Hora and most serious astrology software, compiled to WebAssembly and running on your own device.

Tested against the sky

The test suite checks real events: Mercury's stations of April and May 2024, Saturn entering sidereal Pisces in March 2025, Jupiter entering Gemini in May 2025, and every planet against a second, independent ephemeris.

Your birth data stays home

The chart is worked out in your browser and saved only there. The privacy page lists the only two things that ever leave, and every page has a network log to prove it.

Read every line

All the code, every rule and every reading is public under the AGPL. Disagree with a yoga rule? Open the file and show us.

The pipeline

  1. Your birth details A date, the time the local clock showed, and a place. The place becomes a latitude and longitude, either from the town search or typed by hand.
  2. The clock's offset from Universal Time The time zone comes from the coordinates, using an offline map of zone borders. The offset in force on that date, daylight saving and war time included, comes from the IANA time zone database (the one your phone uses). Before a place kept standard time its clocks followed the Sun, so for those years the offset is the town's own local mean time: its longitude divided by 15, in hours. You can always override the offset by hand.
  3. Universal Time and the Julian Day Local time minus the offset gives Universal Time, which is turned into a Julian Day, a plain count of days used by every ephemeris. The engine also applies ΔT, the gap of about a minute between clock time and the steady time the planets keep, because the Earth's spin is slightly irregular.
  4. Tropical positions from the Swiss Ephemeris For each graha the Swiss Ephemeris returns its geocentric apparent longitude, measured along the ecliptic from the true spring equinox of that date. This is the "tropical" longitude that Western programs print. Dates from 1800 to 2399 use the bundled Swiss Ephemeris files; earlier or later dates use its built-in Moshier model, still accurate to a fraction of an arc second for the planets. Rahu is the Moon's mean node by default (the true node is an option), and Ketu is exactly opposite.
  5. Subtract the ayanamsa The equinox drifts backwards against the stars by about 50 arc seconds a year. The ayanamsa is how far it has drifted, and subtracting it turns a tropical position into a sidereal (Vedic) one. The default is Lahiri (Chitrapaksha), the standard of the Indian government's calendar committee; True Chitra, B.V. Raman and Krishnamurti are also offered.

    One detail most explanations skip: the tropical position is measured from the true equinox, which nods by up to 17 arc seconds on an 18.6-year cycle (nutation). So the amount subtracted is the mean ayanamsa plus that nod. For the sample chart (15 August 1990, 6:30 am, New Delhi) the Sun works out as:

    tropical Sun 141.964948° mean Lahiri ayanamsa − 23.726051° nutation in longitude − 0.004067° (14.6″) ─────────────────────────────────────────── sidereal Sun 118.234830° = Cancer 28°14′05″

    The planet table in the explorer shows this last number, to four decimals, for every planet, so you can compare it with any other program.

  6. The ascendant The degree of the zodiac rising on the eastern horizon at the birth place and moment, from the Swiss Ephemeris house routine, in the same sidereal frame. Houses are whole signs: the rising sign is the first house, the next sign the second, and so on.
  7. Sign, degree, nakshatra and pada Plain arithmetic on the sidereal longitude. The sign is floor(longitude ÷ 30) + 1 and the degree is what is left. Each of the 27 nakshatras spans 13°20′ and each of its four padas 3°20′, so the Sun at 118.2348° is in nakshatra floor(118.2348 ÷ 13.3333) + 1 = 9, Ashlesha, in its fourth pada.
  8. Divisional charts Each of the sixteen vargas, from D1 to D60, divides every sign into equal parts and maps each part to a sign by the rules of Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra. They are all computed from the sidereal longitudes above, so a varga can never disagree with the birth chart's degrees.
  9. Dashas Vimshottari starts from the Moon's nakshatra: how far the Moon has travelled through it sets how much of the first period was already used up at birth. The year length is 365.2425 days. Yogini, Ashtottari and Kalachakra follow their own classical rules from the same Moon.
  10. Strength, yogas, doshas and readings Shadbala, ashtakavarga, the 44 yogas and the 9 doshas are rules applied to the positions above. Each finding is shown with the exact reason it formed or was cancelled, and every rule and reading is a file in the source.

Why your numbers might differ from another program

  • A different ayanamsa. Raman or KP differ from Lahiri by a constant few arc minutes to a degree and a half.
  • Tropical against sidereal. About 24° on every planet means one of the two charts is Western.
  • Mean against true node. Rahu and Ketu can differ by up to about 1°45′.
  • The birth time or place. The ascendant moves about a degree every four minutes, the Moon about half an arc minute every minute.
  • The time zone in old records. Before standard time, or in war years, two programs may read the same clock time differently. The engine shows when it used local mean time.

The check page takes positions pasted from Astro.com, Jagannatha Hora or anything else and shows the difference in arc minutes, with these explanations beside the numbers.

What this does not prove

Accurate positions are astronomy. What they mean is astrology, a traditional practice and not a science, and calculating it carefully does not make a reading come true. Readings and scores show what the tradition says, for reflection, and are not medical, legal or financial advice.
